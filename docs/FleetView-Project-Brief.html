Project Brief · Aug 2026

The fleet stopped living
in WhatsApp.

FleetView is the operating system for a rental fleet: every car, contract, breakdown, garage visit, invoice and repair in one place. Not a dashboard bolted onto a spreadsheet — a workflow engine where a fault is opened, dispatched, repaired, re-inspected and closed by named people with permissions, plus an intelligence layer that reads the free-text history of thousands of past repairs and tells you what is actually wrong with the car and what the garage is about to do about it.

01 — The problem

Where the money and the time were leaking

A rental fleet loses money in three places nobody was measuring: cars sitting in a garage longer than anyone promised, the same fault being paid for twice because it came back, and repairs whose real cost was never attached to the car that caused it. All three were invisible because the process lived in chat messages, phone calls and a maintenance sheet.

⏱

Downtime nobody owned

A car went to a garage and came back "when it came back." No promised date, no clock, no escalation, no record of which stage it was stuck in.

↻

Repairs that came back

The same fault on the same car, fixed twice, paid twice — and no one could see it because the two visits were two unrelated rows.

🧾

Cost with no address

Invoices existed, but nothing bound a dirham to a vehicle, a fault and a garage. So "which cars cost us money" was an opinion.

🎯

One ticket, one truth

A ticket is a container of faults on one car at one garage. Every state change is stamped, attributed to a user, and written to an audit trail. Nothing moves by verbal agreement.

03 — The journey

From "something's wrong" to "back on the road"

Twenty-eight distinct states, but one spine. Notice where the branches are: a diagnosis can end without a ticket, a repair can be rejected at re-inspection, and the car can be released back to rental mid-repair without losing the ticket.

STAGE 0

Something is reported

Driver · Ops · Complaint

A driver requests an inspection, ops logs a customer complaint, or a breakdown comes in from the road. The system stamps where it came from and why as two separate facts — origin ≠ reason. A breakdown immediately grounds the car and forces a QA gate.

STAGE 1

Diagnostic test-drive

Inspector

The inspector drives the car and files findings against a controlled vocabulary — a catalog of selectable faults, not free text. inspection_diagnostic

STAGE 2

Ticket, or no ticket

Inspector

Two exits. Clean car → diagnostic_cleared, closed, no ticket ever existed. Needs work → the ticket opens with its faults attached and severity set, and waits for a dispatch decision. inspection_pending

STAGE 3

Assign the garage

Supervisor

This is where the AI earns its place. The supervisor sees a ranked garage recommendation with the reasoning shown, the expected repair duration, the likely parts, and whether this car has been here for this fault before. They pick the garage, assign a driver, and set the promised return date. awaiting_dispatch

STAGE 4

Pickup and transit

Driver

Odometer captured, garage confirmed, car en route. The downtime clock starts here — and pauses when the car is temporarily released back to rental. in_transit → under_repair

STAGE 5

Inside the workshop

Supervisor · Garage

Checkpoints with evidence keep the ticket alive: what's been done, what's waiting on a part, what changed about the promise. Silence escalates on its own — the reminder gets louder as the promised date approaches and passes. Parts requested here become purchases, and purchases become installed components on that specific car.

STAGE 6

The garage says it's done

Garage → Supervisor

Not accepted on faith. Repair review, then ready_for_reinspection. Each fault on the ticket is individually resolved: fixed (with evidence), not found (the workshop checked, it doesn't exist), or cancelled. Those three outcomes are different data, not different words.

STAGE 7

Re-inspection — the QC gate

Inspector

The inspector drives it again. Pass → the service log is written and the ticket moves on. Fail → reinspection_failed, straight back to the supervisor for re-dispatch, and that round trip is now permanently on the garage's record as a comeback.

STAGE 8

Back in the fleet

Driver · Finance

Driver collects from the garage, odometer captured again, car returns to the pool. The ticket stays open through awaiting_invoice until the bill lands and the cost is attributed to the vehicle and the fault — then closed.

The branch that matters most: a failed re-inspection is not an embarrassment to be hidden — it's the single most valuable signal in the system. It's what turns "which garage should we use" from a relationship question into a measured one.
04 — Intelligence

The AI reads the fault, and predicts the repair

Years of maintenance history exist as free text: mechanic shorthand, mixed Arabic and English, abbreviations, misspellings. Useless to a human at scale, and useless to a database. The intelligence layer turns that text into structured knowledge — and then uses it to answer the three questions a supervisor actually asks.

STEP 01

Read the text

Every historical repair line, invoice remark and inspection note is scanned against a curated fault ontology — 15 domains (engine, brakes, electrical, HVAC, transmission, suspension…) each holding concepts and the real-world terms mechanics actually type.

STEP 02

Name the fault

Text becomes a concept: "check-engine light", "A/C not cooling", "airbag warning". Crucially the engine also separates a service (oil change) from a fault (oil leak) from mere context — counting a routine service as a breakdown poisons every metric downstream.

STEP 03

Build the signature

A repair becomes a signature: this fault, on this model, at this garage, for this duration, at this cost, with these parts. Signatures are what make two visits three years apart comparable.

STEP 04

Answer the question

Given a new ticket, find similar past repairs and return the honest distribution: median and p90 duration, cost range, the parts that were actually used, and which garage resolved it first time.

🩺

"What is actually wrong with this car?"

Recurring-fault detection chains repeat visits on the same vehicle into a single story. If a car has come back for the same electrical fault three times in eight months, that's not three tickets — that's one unresolved problem with a pattern, and it's escalated as one. A per-vehicle Suggested Checks list is generated from its own repeat faults, so the next inspection starts already knowing the car's history.

🏭

"What will the garage do, and how long?"

Before dispatch, the system predicts the repair: expected duration from real cohort statistics, likely parts, expected cost band, and a ranked garage recommendation scored on measured first-time-fix rate, comeback rate, turnaround and cost — weighted by how critical the fault is. The supervisor sees the score and the reasoning behind it, on one screen, before committing the car.

It speaks operations, not engine

A hard rule in this codebase: the engine emits reason codes, never English sentences. The interface renders them as plain operational language — "this garage fixed this fault first time on 8 of the last 9 cars" — with the technical scoring folded away under Technical details. 📐

It shows its work — and its limits

Every recommendation is backed by an explainability graph: the nodes and evidence that produced the number, inspectable from the UI. And the platform grades its own confidence — where the history is thin, it says so rather than inventing certainty. A backtest harness scores the predictor against what actually happened afterwards.

ExplanationEngineConfidenceScorerintelligence:backtest
An honest note on the AI: the fault ontology ships seeded and deterministic — the matching runs in plain code with no API key and no per-request model call, which is why it is fast, auditable and reproducible. The measured concept recall is high, but the coverage report is explicit that a large share of historical records are cosmetic rather than mechanical, and that predictive confidence scoring has not yet earned the right to be shown as a number. That gate is deliberate: the platform doesn't display a score it can't defend.

💡 Worth showing stakeholders

Three screens tell the whole story better than any deck: the ticket detail with its live audit trail; the dispatch step where the garage recommendation and its reasoning appear side by side; and a vehicle timeline where a car's entire life — rentals, faults, repairs, costs, odometer — reads as one continuous investigation.

🧭 The principle underneath all of it

Map the data as it is; don't infer what you can't prove. Every field is labelled a fact, a derivation, or a judgement. Every screen shows its origin. Where the evidence is thin, the platform says so out loud. That restraint is why the numbers can be trusted at all.
